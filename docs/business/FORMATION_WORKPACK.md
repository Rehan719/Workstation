## Route 1. Staying unincorporated while the offer is tested (a sole trader)

- A sole trader works for themselves, is classed as self-employed and makes all the business decisions; it is the simplest structure to set up and keep records for. [observed: GOV.UK, Become a sole trader]
- The owner is personally responsible for all of the business's debts, which the page calls unlimited liability. [observed: GOV.UK, Become a sole trader]
- Trading can start straight away without registering, but a sole trader must register for Self Assessment if earning more than £1,000 in a tax year (6 April to 5 April). [observed; SOURCED: https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader, read 2026-11-20]
- A trading name or one's own name may be used, and records are kept from the start for the Self Assessment return. [observed: GOV.UK, Become a sole trader]
- Moving later from a sole trader to a limited company is usually easier than moving the other way. [observed: GOV.UK, Set up a business]
- A business partnership is the same unincorporated route shared between people: each personally shares responsibility for its losses and bills, and one is nominated to manage its tax returns with HMRC. It needs a second person, so it is listed for completeness only. [observed: GOV.UK, Set up a business partnership]

## Route 2. A company limited by shares

- A limited company is legally separate from the people who own it and is run by one or more directors; its owners are responsible for its debts only up to the value of their investment. [observed: GOV.UK, Set up a private limited company]
- It is registered before it starts trading, and may then stay dormant. Its directors keep company and accounting records, confirm Companies House's information in a confirmation statement, and file accounts and tax returns; they stay legally responsible even when they hire help. [observed: GOV.UK, Set up a private limited company]
- The company pays Corporation Tax on its profits and registers for VAT if it meets the requirements; people who take money out of it may pay tax on that income. [observed: GOV.UK, Set up a private limited company]
- A company limited by shares raises investment by selling shares, is owned by its shareholders and pays them profits as dividends; one person can own the whole company and be its only director. [observed: GOV.UK, Set up a private limited company]
- Its name cannot be the same as a registered company's name and usually ends in Limited or Ltd. [observed: GOV.UK, Set up a private limited company]
- A company limited by guarantee is usually a non-profit or a charity: guarantors instead of shareholders, and profits invested back into the company instead of paid to its owners. [observed: GOV.UK, Set up a private limited company]

## Route 3. A community interest company (CIC)

- A CIC is a limited company whose purpose is primarily community benefit rather than private profit, with a company's separate legal identity; its directors can be paid or unpaid. [observed: GOV.UK, Community Interest Companies Guidance]
- It must satisfy the Regulator, and keep satisfying it for its whole life, that a reasonable person might consider its activities are carried on for the benefit of the community; certain political activities are excluded. [observed: GOV.UK, Community Interest Companies Guidance]
- Every CIC has a compulsory asset lock that cannot be removed, so that its assets and profits are used for the community; the guidance says this has permanent long-term consequences. [observed: GOV.UK, Community Interest Companies Guidance]
- Its directors file an annual CIC report (form CIC34) with the accounts, even while it is dormant. [observed: GOV.UK, Community Interest Companies Guidance]
- A CIC is either limited by shares, and may then pay dividends within a cap of no more than 35% of its profits, or limited by guarantee; once incorporated, one cannot be converted into the other. [observed: GOV.UK, Community Interest Companies Guidance]

## Route 4. A charitable or waqf-oriented arrangement: a later structure needing qualified advice

- A charity needs charitable purposes for the public benefit (the page's list includes education and religion), usually at least three trustees, a name, a structure and a governing document, and it registers if its annual income is over £5,000 or it is a charitable incorporated organisation. [observed; SOURCED: https://www.gov.uk/setting-up-charity, read 2026-11-20]
- The four main charity structures are a charitable incorporated organisation (CIO), a charitable company limited by guarantee, an unincorporated association and a trust; a corporate structure can contract, employ and hold property in its own name and its trustees generally are not personally liable, while an unincorporated charity's trustees are. [observed: Charity Commission, Charity types: how to choose a structure (CC22a)]
- A charitable company cannot distribute its surpluses to its members and can only apply its assets to its charitable purposes. [observed: Charity Commission, Charity types: how to choose a structure (CC22a)]
- A waqf-oriented arrangement: no official page on one was read, so how such an endowment would be held in the law of England and Wales is NOT CHECKED. It would need a solicitor's advice, and its religious character is for a qualified scholar; this document says nothing about either. [observed]
- Because a charity's assets may serve only its purposes, charitable status would change what the platform's own economy may do; that is why the plan names this route as a later structure, not a first one. [inference]

## The routes compared

| Criterion | 1. Sole trader | 2. Company limited by shares | 3. CIC | 4. Charity, or a waqf-oriented arrangement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A legal person of its own | No | Yes | Yes | A CIO or a charitable company: yes. A trust or an association: no. A waqf: NOT CHECKED |
| Liability for the debts | The owner's, unlimited | Up to the investment | Up to the investment or the guarantee | Corporate: trustees generally not liable. Unincorporated: trustees liable |
| Registered before trading | No; Self Assessment above a threshold | Yes, at Companies House | Yes, at Companies House, and the community interest test is met | With the Charity Commission above an income threshold, or as a CIO |
| Profit paid to its owners | Yes, after tax | Yes, as dividends | Only within the dividend cap, and only if limited by shares | No |
| Changing course later | Usually easiest to a company | NOT CHECKED here | The asset lock cannot be removed | NOT CHECKED here |
